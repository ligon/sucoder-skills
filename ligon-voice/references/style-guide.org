#+TITLE: Style Guide: Replicating the Ligon Voice
#+AUTHOR: Synthesized from 18 .org research documents
#+DATE: 2026-02-27

* Overview

  This guide distills the prose habits found across 18 research
  documents spanning development economics, microeconomic theory,
  demand estimation, risk-sharing, and editorial correspondence.  The
  voice belongs to a senior academic economist who writes with unusual
  clarity and personality for the genre---technically rigorous yet
  approachable, authoritative yet self-aware.

* 1. Syntax and Sentence Architecture

** 1.1 Sentence Length: High Variance as Strategy

   Average sentence length falls in the 25--35 word range, but the
   /variance/ is the defining feature.  Long, heavily subordinated
   sentences (40--80+ words) alternate with short, punchy ones (4--15
   words).  The short sentences are never filler; they serve as
   rhetorical resets after complex passages.

   - *Long build-up, short landing:*  A paragraph might spend three
     sentences laying out qualifications and conditions, then close
     with a direct, even blunt declaration.

   *Examples:*
   - Short: "How can one make sense of this?"
   - Short: "That is the task we undertake here."
   - Long: "Precisely /because/ weather is important, farmers are
     likely to have found other ways to at least partially address the
     risks they face with respect to variation in crop income."

   *Rule:* After any passage exceeding ~50 words of continuous
   qualification, follow with a sentence under 15 words that
   reorients the reader.

** 1.2 Clause Stacking and Qualification

   The primary syntactic pattern is a main declarative clause followed
   by one or more subordinate clauses that qualify, condition, or
   extend it.  Common structures:

   - "We find X, but only Y."
   - "This is [positive assessment], but it comes at [cost]."
   - "[Standard claim]. However, [complication that motivates the paper]."

   Qualifying phrases accumulate through relative clauses,
   participial phrases, and parenthetical asides (using em-dashes,
   parentheses, or semicolons).  Each subordinate clause adds exactly
   one new condition or piece of information.

   *Rule:* Never let a subordinate clause introduce more than one new
   idea.  If it does, split the sentence.

** 1.3 Semicolon-Heavy List Constructions

   When presenting multiple related points, the preferred structure
   is a single sentence with items joined by semicolons:

   #+begin_quote
   "The approach imposes fewer restrictions on the demand system than
   is usual; avoids the usual sausage factory from which consumption
   aggregates are extruded; should allow for much less expensive data
   collection; and directly yields measures of both household marginal
   utility and functions of shadow prices which can be used in
   subsequent analysis and model testing."
   #+end_quote

   *Rule:* For lists of 3--5 conceptually parallel claims, use a
   single semicolon-joined sentence rather than bullet points or
   separate sentences.

** 1.4 Passive Voice: Moderate and Purposeful

   Passive voice appears mainly for procedural descriptions
   ("Randomization was done stratifying on four binary household
   characteristics") and when the agent is irrelevant.  Active voice
   dominates everywhere else.  The author never hides behind passives
   to avoid ownership of claims.

* 2. Lexicon

** 2.1 Three Registers, Freely Mixed

   The vocabulary draws simultaneously from three registers:

   1. *Technical economics:*  "marginal utility of expenditures,"
      "Engel curves," "Euler equation," "Frischian demands,"
      "complementary slackness," "arbitrage," "CRRA," "Pareto
      efficient."

   2. *Development policy:* "ultra-poor," "graduation model,"
      "unconditional cash transfers," "targeting effectiveness,"
      "food security," "lean season," "enumerators."

   3. *Plain conversational English:* "think about" (not "consider"),
      "look at" (not "examine"), "pin down" (not "determine"),
      "a bunch of," "finicky," "awesome," "old chestnut."

   *Rule:* Use technical vocabulary without apology when it is
   precise.  But surround it with plain, conversational connecting
   prose.  Never stack jargon terms without bridging them with
   everyday language.

** 2.2 Contractions: A Signature Feature

   Contractions appear freely throughout---even in formal journal
   prose.  This is perhaps the single most distinctive lexical
   feature.  "it's," "doesn't," "can't," "we've," "needn't,"
   "won't" all appear in published or submission-ready work.

   *Rule:* Use contractions wherever the uncontracted form would
   sound stilted.  Avoid them only in the most formal summary
   statements (e.g., the final sentence of an abstract).

** 2.3 Hedging: Measured, Not Timid

   Claims are bounded but not weakened.  Preferred hedges:

   - "it seems difficult to justify"
   - "perhaps surprisingly"
   - "at least sometimes"
   - "is thought to be"
   - "it's been surprising to discover"
   - "we cannot rule out the possibility"
   - "it seems reasonable to suppose"

   *Rule:* Hedge empirical generalizations.  Do not hedge deductive
   results or well-established facts.  The theoretical voice is
   markedly more assertive than the empirical voice.

** 2.4 Transitions

   Standard academic transitions are used, but sparingly:
   "However," "In particular," "Moreover," "Accordingly," "In other
   words."  The author also favors "Now consider..." and "To see
   this..." as mathematical-expository transitions borrowed from
   proof-writing.

   *Rule:* No more than one explicit transition word per paragraph.
   Let the logic do most of the connecting.

* 3. Tone

** 3.1 The Dominant Tone: Authoritative Teacher

   The voice is that of a senior professor explaining to a smart
   colleague or advanced student.  It is authoritative without being
   pompous.  The author frequently pauses to build intuition before
   presenting formalism:

   - "The intuition is simple:"
   - "Some discussion of the different elements of this expression is
     called for."
   - "To build intuition, we first present a simplified model."

   *Rule:* Before any substantial equation or formal result, provide
   at least one sentence of plain-English motivation.  After it,
   provide at least one sentence explaining what was just shown and
   why it matters.

** 3.2 Constructive Skepticism

   The characteristic rhetorical posture is gentle correction.  The
   structure is:

   1. Acknowledge the prior literature or conventional approach with
      genuine respect.
   2. Describe it carefully.
   3. Reveal its problems through concrete consequences, often by
      showing the approach leads to an absurd result.
   4. Present the alternative.

   #+begin_quote
   "This is an admirable reduction in complexity, but it comes at a
   high price."
   #+end_quote

   #+begin_quote
   "The standard approach suggests that droughts, floods, and pests
   are beneficial."
   #+end_quote

   *Rule:* Never dismiss prior work.  Instead, show what it implies
   and let the reader see the problem.  Let absurdity speak for
   itself.

** 3.3 First-Person Usage

   - "We" is the default for co-authored work and for the
     pedagogical "we" that includes the reader ("we need to think
     about...").
   - "I" appears in single-author work and in editorial
     correspondence.
   - Working notes and internal documents use "I" freely and shift
     to a noticeably more informal register.

   *Rule:* Use "we" in formal prose even when sole-authored, except
   when expressing a genuinely personal judgment or in editorial
   letters.

** 3.4 Intellectual Generosity

   The author engages with interlocutors---referees, co-authors,
   the literature---with evident respect and even warmth: "I mainly
   want to say how very much I appreciate the careful work of the
   technical referee."  Disagreement is framed as complementary
   rather than adversarial.

   *Rule:* When disagreeing with prior work, frame it as "X is
   excellent, and by extending it we find Y," not "X is wrong."

* 4. Metaphor and Figurative Language

** 4.1 Sparing but Vivid

   Figurative language is rare---perhaps 2--3 metaphors per
   paper---but when deployed it is memorable and slightly wry:

   - *The sausage factory:* "constructing such aggregates may be
     rather like making sausage" / "the usual sausage factory from
     which consumption aggregates are extruded."  (A sustained
     metaphor for messy data aggregation.)
   - *Heroic assumptions / Additional heroics:* Extending the stock
     phrase with irony---the "heroism" is absurd rather than
     admirable.
   - *"Waded through":*  A physical metaphor for laborious reading
     of a proof.
   - *"A litany of shocks":*  Quasi-religious connotation of
     relentless repetition.
   - *"Leveraged bets":*  Reframing farmers' storage decisions in
     capital-markets language.
   - *"Sell low and buy high":*  Inverting the familiar investing
     maxim.
   - *"Low bar":*  A colloquial metaphor embedded in formal prose.
   - *"Viewed through this lens":*  A conventional academic
     metaphor, used without irony.

   *Rule:* Deploy at most one or two vivid metaphors per paper.
   Prefer metaphors borrowed from adjacent domains (finance,
   manufacturing, physical effort).  Never use extended similes or
   literary conceits.  Let the metaphor do its work in a single
   clause, then return to precision.

** 4.2 Dead Metaphors Used Naturally

   Standard economics metaphors ("smooth consumption," "hedge
   against risk," "span the state space") are used as natural
   vocabulary rather than as conscious figures of speech.  No need to
   call attention to them.

** 4.3 Occasional Wordplay

   Light puns and double meanings appear when they arise naturally:

   #+begin_quote
   "This is an admirable reduction in complexity, but it comes at a
   high price.  In this case the price is paid when one assumes the
   indirect utility function and index allow the equivalence."
   #+end_quote

   Here "price" does double duty---ordinary English and technical
   economics.

   *Rule:* If a word naturally has both a technical and ordinary
   meaning in context, let the ambiguity stand without drawing
   attention to it.  Never force a pun.

* 5. Rhetorical Devices

** 5.1 Rhetorical Questions as Section Openers

   Papers and sections frequently open with a question:
   - "What intervention(s) effectively alleviate poverty?"
   - "What happens when you give an 'ultra-poor' household a
     productive asset?"
   - "What's an asset?"
   - "Is it realistic to think of farmers taking short asset
     positions?"
   - "How can one make sense of this?"

   *Rule:* Use one rhetorical question to open a paper or major
   section.  Follow it immediately with the beginning of an answer.
   Do not use rhetorical questions mid-paragraph or in clusters.

** 5.2 Anticipation of Objections

   The author regularly surfaces the reader's likely objection,
   then disarms it:

   #+begin_quote
   "It may seem somewhat unrealistic to think of the farmer taking
   short positions in some assets.  [...] But taking a short credit
   position is simply the act of borrowing; taking a short land
   position can be accomplished by simply leasing out one's land;
   and both of these are financial behaviors very commonly observed."
   #+end_quote

   *Rule:* When presenting a counterintuitive claim, voice the
   objection in the reader's likely words, then answer it concretely.

** 5.3 Parenthetical Asides for Thoroughness

   Heavy use of parenthetical commentary to anticipate reader
   questions and demonstrate thoroughness:
   - "(and most in the region)"
   - "(whether of vegetables or maize)"
   - "(a fedan is 4200 m^2, slightly more than an acre)"
   - "(mathematically accurate, but devoid of intuition)"

   *Rule:* Use parentheticals to add a quick factual gloss or
   qualification.  Do not use them for entire arguments---that
   belongs in the main text or a footnote.

** 5.4 Footnotes for Nuance

   Substantive qualifications, secondary arguments, and caveats are
   systematically placed in footnotes, keeping the primary argument
   stream clean.

   *Rule:* If a qualification runs longer than one clause, move it to
   a footnote.

* 6. Structural Patterns

** 6.1 Opening Pattern

   Papers typically begin with:
   1. A broad declarative statement about the world.
   2. Immediately followed by "but" / "however" / a question that
      introduces the paper's distinctive contribution.

   #+begin_quote
   "Consumption is thought to be highly seasonal for poor farmers
   in developing countries... It is also well documented that
   farmers sell their harvests during peak seasons and at least
   sometimes buy back those crops at higher prices... [But] a
   simple model of intertemporal consumption smoothing raises
   important questions about these results."
   #+end_quote

   *Rule:* Open with what is known, then pivot to what is missing.
   The pivot should arrive within the first paragraph.

** 6.2 Temporal Precision

   The writing is meticulous about establishing when events occurred
   relative to each other: "6-8 months after the asset transfers,"
   "15-18 months after the asset transfers," "about 6 months after
   transfer."

   *Rule:* Always specify temporal relationships with concrete
   intervals.  "Later" and "subsequently" are insufficient.

** 6.3 Serial Qualification of Results

   Results are presented with cascading specificity: direction of
   effect, magnitude, statistical significance, then comparison to
   other treatments---often within a single sentence.

   *Rule:* When reporting an empirical result, state the direction
   first, then the magnitude, then the significance, then the
   context.

* 7. Dry Humor

  A thread of understated, wry humor runs through even the most
  formal passages.  It is never broad, always in service of the
  argument, and arrives without warning:

  - "Since CRRA is a special case of CFE one can't really do worse!"
  - "Heroic assumptions are typically required to value flows of
    services.  Additional heroics are required to construct price
    indices."
  - "What would be awesome would be to be able to do this all via
    simple linear regression."
  - Fisher "offers the coinage 'wantab.'"

  *Rule:* Humor should be dry and arise from the material itself.
  Never signal that you are being funny.  The exclamation mark is
  permitted once per paper, at most.

* 9. Opportunities for Improvement

  The strengths described above occasionally work against clarity.
  The following are areas where discipline would sharpen the prose.

** 9.1 Front-Load the Contribution

   The constructive-skepticism pattern (respectfully set up the
   conventional approach, describe it, /then/ reveal its problems)
   is intellectually honest but can delay the paper's contribution
   by a full page.  A reader skimming the introduction may never
   reach the pivot.

   *Revision strategy:* State the punchline in the first
   paragraph---"We show X; this matters because the standard
   approach implies Y"---and /then/ walk through the conventional
   approach to show why.  The skepticism still lands, but the reader
   knows where you are headed.

** 9.2 Impose a Soft Ceiling on Sentence Length

   The long tail of sentence length sometimes defeats itself.  When
   a sentence embeds three or more subordinate clauses, the main
   verb's relationship to its subject becomes hard to reconstruct.

   #+begin_quote
   "The approach imposes fewer restrictions on the demand system than
   is usual; avoids the usual sausage factory from which consumption
   aggregates are extruded; should allow for much less expensive data
   collection; and directly yields measures of both household marginal
   utility and functions of shadow prices which can be used in
   subsequent analysis and model testing."
   #+end_quote

   By the fourth semicolon-clause the reader must re-parse what "the
   approach" is doing.

   *Revision strategy:* Apply a soft ceiling of ~50 words per
   sentence, violated only when the structure is genuinely parallel
   and each clause is short.  When a semicolon-list exceeds four
   items, break it into two sentences or use an actual list.

** 9.3 Limit Parenthetical Interruptions

   Parenthetical asides demonstrate thoroughness but fracture the
   main clause.  Compare:

   #+begin_quote
   "It is common in this community (and most in the region) to store
   non-perishable food like maize, cassava, or millet (whether dried
   or fresh) as a form of savings."
   #+end_quote

   vs.

   #+begin_quote
   "It is common in this community to store non-perishable food as a
   form of savings---maize, cassava, or millet, dried or fresh.
   Most communities in the region do the same."
   #+end_quote

   The second version delivers the same information without forcing
   the reader to maintain a stack of open parentheses.

   *Revision strategy:* At most one parenthetical per sentence.
   When a second is needed, promote one to its own clause, an
   em-dash aside, or a footnote.

** 9.4 Guard Against Hedge Accumulation

   Individual hedges are well-calibrated, but they sometimes pile up
   across a paragraph until the net claim is unclear.  A passage
   might contain "is thought to be," "at least sometimes," "at least
   partially," and "we cannot rule out" within a few lines.  Each is
   defensible; together they leave the reader unsure what is actually
   being claimed.

   *Revision strategy:* State the claim cleanly once, then qualify
   it once.  One hedge per claim.  If the evidence genuinely
   supports only a weak statement, say so directly ("The evidence is
   thin") rather than hedging the hedge.

** 9.5 Let the Notes Voice Survive into the Paper

   The working notes (e.g., =remote_survey_notes.org=, internal
   VESDemand notes) use a direct, first-person voice---"I don't
   have a particularly satisfying explanation for this"---that is
   disarmingly clear.  Some of that directness gets formalized away
   in revision.  Sentences like "Is it realistic to think of farmers
   taking short asset positions?  That's a behavior we tend to
   associate with sophisticated investors" are among the clearest in
   the corpus precisely because they read like the notes voice, not
   the journal voice.

   *Revision strategy:* During revision, compare a polished
   paragraph to its notes-stage ancestor.  If the notes version is
   clearer, keep it.  The contraction habit already resists
   over-formalization; push further.

** 9.6 Add Signposts Within Sections

   The papers use roadmap paragraphs at the end of introductions
   ("Section 2 does X, Section 3 does Y"), but within sections the
   argument sometimes proceeds as a continuous flow without
   sub-signposting.

   *Revision strategy:* At the top of any derivation or argument
   spanning more than a page, add a single orienting sentence: "We
   need three results to establish the main proposition."  This
   dramatically reduces cognitive load, especially in long
   theoretical sections, and costs almost nothing in word count.

* 10. The Voice in a Sentence

  Write as a senior professor who respects the reader's intelligence,
  explains before formalizing, hedges only where evidence is
  genuinely uncertain, lets absurd implications speak for themselves,
  and occasionally allows a wry aside to surface---always in service
  of the argument, never for its own sake.  State the punchline
  early, qualify once rather than thrice, and let the directness of
  the working-note voice survive into the final draft.
